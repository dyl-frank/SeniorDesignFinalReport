%  NEUTRONICS ANALYSES
% ============================================================

The preliminary scoping studies in Section~\ref{sec:scoping-section} established the physical basis for the LBR design and confirmed feasibility at the level of 0-D infinite lattice calculations. This section presents the high-fidelity neutronics analyses that address the four limitations identified at the close of that section: isotopic validation against measured data, replacement of the infinite lattice approximation with a full assembly geometry, spectral coupling effects, and axial power redistribution. The analyses proceed in three stages --- (1)~benchmarking against TMI-1 post-irradiation examination data, (2)~moderator and pitch confirmation using a full Serpent lattice model, and (3)~iterative design optimization --- each building directly on the scoping conclusions.

All detailed neutronics analyses were performed using the Serpent~2 Monte Carlo neutron and photon transport code \cite{Leppanen2025Status2024}. Serpent was selected for its advanced coupled burnup capabilities, including: burnup-dependent geometry evolution, detailed isotopic and toxicity tracking, flexible depletion zone subdivision, universe nesting with reflective symmetry options, efficient parallel processing via domain decomposition, material definition transfer via binary restart files, fine control of numerical solvers (CRAM, TTA, and substep methods), and a comprehensive Python-based output-parsing library (SerpentTools) \cite{Johnson2020SerpentTools:Serpent}. These features are essential for the multi-physics demands of the LBR workflow, where spatially resolved spent fuel compositions must be reliably propagated across successive simulation stages --- a requirement the 0-D scoping tools could not meet.

\subsubsection{Operating History and Depletion Solver Stability}
% ============================================================

Burnup simulations followed the irradiation history of Assembly NJ05YU as documented in \cite{Wolf2000AnalysisReport}, comprising: a 666-day first cycle with control rods fully inserted, a 36-day shutdown period, a 692-day second cycle with control rods fully withdrawn, and a 20 year cool-down period. A constant assembly fission power of 14.508\,MW was maintained throughout all operating periods. The assembly-averaged discharge burnup is approximately 40.1\,GWd/t.

Two independent simulations of the operating history were performed, each using different numerical solver configurations in order to assess the sensitivity of isotopic predictions to solver parameters. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:tmi-stability}, solver selection has a non-trivial effect on the predicted nuclide evolution, particularly for short-lived intermediaries and actinide chains that are sensitive to numerical stiffness. The stable configuration, characterized by three substep divisions and smaller timesteps, was adopted for all subsequent material generation.

\subsubsection{Isotopic Validation Against Experimental Data}
\label{sec:isotopic-validation}

% ============================================================

Spatial isotopic distributions predicted by the Serpent model were validated against radiochemical assay measurements of Assembly NJ05YU, originally reported in 1999 and subsequently re-evaluated in 2013  \cite{Wolf2000AnalysisReport}. Validation focused on the distribution of $^{235}$U and $^{239}$Pu as the presence of these isotopes has the most significant influence on LBR's performance.

The simulated axial distribution of $^{235}$U concentration is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:U235_evol_tmi} alongside the experimental assay data. Simulation results fall within the $2\sigma$ uncertainty bounds of the measured data across the full active length, providing high confidence in the depletion model's treatment of $^{235}$U burnout.

\subsection{Moderator and Lattice Pitch Confirmation (D. Frank)}
% ============================================================

The 0-D scoping study (\S~\ref{sec:scoping}) identified D$_2$O moderation at a 6\,cm lattice pitch as the design point on the basis of an infinite lattice model with a single tabulated spent fuel composition. That study could not account for the spectral effects of the full assembly geometry, the axially resolved burnup profile from the TMI-1 results established in. An assembly-level parametric pitch sweep was therefore conducted using the TMI-1 results in \S~\ref{sec:isotopic-validation} to confirm (or revise) the scoping selection under physically realistic conditions.

Spent fuel material definitions were transferred directly from the validated TMI-1 model via binary restart file, without fuel shuffling, preserving the full axially resolved nuclide inventory for each depletion zone. Using H$_2$O and D$_2$O as moderator candidates, optimization was conducted around the initial reactivity and burn-to-breed ratio of $^{239}$Pu -- as was done in the 0-D scoping calculations -- results are shown in Figures~\ref{fig:keff_sweep} and~\ref{fig:b2b_sweep} respectively.

\subsection{Core Design Iteration and Optimization (D. Frank)}
% ============================================================

Examination of Figures~\ref{fig:U235_evol_tmi} and~\ref{fig:Pu239_evol_tmi} reveals a characteristic feature of PWR spent fuel: the upper and lower $\sim$50\,cm of each fuel rod retain a notably higher inventory of fissile material ($^{235}$U and $^{239}$Pu) relative to the axial midplane, due to flux depression by the adjacent structural hardware and control rod shadowing effects during the first irradiation cycle. In the baseline LBR configuration, these under-depleted end regions represent an underutilized fissile resource. Design iteration was therefore aimed at redistributing fission power toward the rod extremities to more efficiently exploit this inventory.

Design performance was assessed using two figures of merit evaluated at the end of the critical cycle:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Critical cycle length}: the duration over which $k_\text{eff} \geq 1$ is maintained, serving as a proxy for energy extraction and economic viability.
    \item \textbf{Coefficient of variation (CV) of fissile isotope distribution}: a dimensionless measure of axial uniformity in the fissile inventory at end-of-life, defined as:
\end{enumerate}

\begin{equation}
    \text{CV} = \frac{s}{\bar{w}} \times 100\%
    \label{eq:cv}
\end{equation}

\noindent where the sample mean $\bar{w}$ and standard deviation $s$ of the axial fissile weight fraction distribution are:

\begin{equation}
    \bar{w} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i, \qquad
    s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N-1} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left(w_i - \bar{w}\right)^2}
    \label{eq:cv_components}
\end{equation}

\noindent Here, $N$ is the number of equally sized axial depletion nodes, and $w_i$ is the total fissile weight percent (summed over $^{233}$U, $^{235}$U, $^{239}${Pu}, and $^{241}$Pu) at node $i$. A lower CV at end-of-life indicates a more axially uniform depletion profile, which is beneficial from both neutronics and fuel management perspectives, as it implies more complete utilization of the available fissile inventory and reduced peak-to-average power factors. Note that this metric is valid only for a uniform axial discretization in which each node $w_i$ represents an equal-length segment of the active fuel column.

% ============================================================
\subsubsection{Design Variants}
% ============================================================

Four design variants were evaluated, each representing a different strategy for redistributing fission power toward the rod extremities. The initial design parameters were more-or-less arbitrarily chosen and illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:4-designs-sketch}.

\paragraph{Baseline (Spent Fuel As-Is).}
In this configuration, the LBR assembly is loaded exclusively with spent PWR fuel rods in their as-discharged condition, with no additional devices or structural modifications. This case serves as the reference against which all modified designs are compared.

\paragraph{Beryllium Caps.}
In this design, each fuel rod is fitted with beryllium ($^9$Be) axial reflector plugs in the upper and lower 70\,cm of the active zone.
Beryllium is an effective fast-neutron reflector and moderator due to its low neutron capture cross section and significant scattering cross section, and it is a neutron multiplier via the $^9$Be$(n,2n)$ reaction. The intent is to reflect neutrons that would otherwise stream axially out of the assembly back into the fissile end regions, thereby increasing the local thermal flux and enhancing fission rates in the less-depleted rod extremities.

\paragraph{Fueled Power Inverter (PI-F).}
In this design, every alternate lattice position is occupied by a fresh fuel rod, occupying 25/225 of the lattice positions as shown in. These rods have a graded enrichment profile: the central region (coinciding with the high-burnup zone of adjacent spent rods) is loaded with 3.5\,wt.\%\,UO$_2$, while the end regions are loaded with 4.5\,wt.\%\,UO$_2$ to compensate for the lower reactivity in the axial periphery and to promote peaked fission power at the rod extremities. This design introduces fresh fissile material as an active power-shaping tool.

\paragraph{Inert Power Inverter (PI-I)}
In this design, every alternate lattice position is occupied by a novel component designated the \textit{Power Inverter}. In the reference Power Inverter configuration, the upper and lower 70\,cm of each device contain 5\,wt.\%\,UO$_2$ fuel pins, while the central region is occupied by cerium dioxide (CeO$_2$, ceria) pins --- all enclosed within a shared Zircaloy-4 cladding tube. The Power Inverter is designed to invert the axial power profile relative to the baseline: fission is concentrated in the end regions (co-located with the under-depleted extremities of the surrounding spent rods), while the central region contributes negligible fission power.

Ceria was selected as the central inert fill material for several reasons: (i) it is largely transparent to thermal neutrons due to its low absorption cross section, allowing the axial neutron flux to diffuse into the end fuel regions without being parasitically absorbed; (ii) it is chemically inert and thermally stable at LWR operating temperatures; and (iii) it shares closely matched thermo-mechanical properties with UO$_2$ --- including crystal structure (fluorite), thermal conductivity, and coefficient of thermal expansion --- which mitigates stress concentration and material compatibility concerns at the CeO$_2$/UO$_2$ interface.

Beyond the neutronics considerations, the selection of the best design is ultimately limited by reactor safety. The NRC requires a maximum power peaking factor of \textbf{SOURCE} -- allowing the reactor to operate at a higher power density. PPF is calculated in the following manner:

\begin{equation}
    \text{PPF} = \frac{\max_{i,j,k}\left(q_{i,j,k}^{\prime\prime\prime}\right)}{\dfrac{1}{N}\displaystyle\sum_{i,j,k} q_{i,j,k}^{\prime\prime\prime}}\quad,
\end{equation}

where indices $i,j,k$ represent a location within the assembly, $q'''$ is the power density in W/g, and $N$ is the number of sample points.

The power distributions and power peaking factor results for the four designs is shown in Figure \ref{fig:ppf-4designs}.

The \textit{Fueled Power Inverter} design achieves the highest weighted score (2.75), driven primarily by its superior critical cycle length. \subsubsection{Fresh Fuel Rod Enrichment Optimization}
% ============================================================

Having selected the \textit{Fueled Power Inverter} design as the preferred concept, a parametric sweep was conducted to identify the optimal combination of the two free enrichment parameters: the central region enrichment (co-located with the high-burnup midplane of adjacent spent rods) and the edge region enrichment (co-located with the under-depleted extremities). Because each high-fidelity full-assembly simulation requires in excess of 24 hours of wall-clock time, a computationally reduced model with periodic boundary conditions was constructed, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:simplified_lbr}. This reduces runtime to under one hour per case, enabling a broad sweep at the cost of geometric fidelity and accurate PPF prediction.
