% ============================================================
%  CONCLUSIONS AND PRACTICAL SUGGESTIONS
% ============================================================

This report has presented the full design and analysis of the Last Breath
Reactor, from initial transmutation kinetics through Monte Carlo depletion,
thermal-hydraulic and structural qualification, and economic benchmarking.
All three primary design objectives have been satisfied at the analysis
level attained:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Objective 1 (Nonproliferation)}: Per-assembly $^{239}$Pu
    inventory reduced by 36.6\% (2.41\,$\to$\,1.53\,kg), exceeding the
    $\geq$20\% target; $^{240}$Pu fraction increased from 25.6\% to 33.4\%,
    degrading weapons attractiveness.
    \item \textbf{Objective 2 (Sustained criticality)}: $k_\text{eff} \geq 1$
    maintained to 60.1\,GWd/tHM with the Fueled Power Inverter, exceeding
    the $\geq$60\,GWd/t target.
    \item \textbf{Objective 3 (Thermal safety)}: DNBR$_\text{peak}$ = 2.95
    ($>$1.3 minimum); average-channel cladding and centerline temperatures
    within regulatory limits; PPF = 2.23 ($\leq$2.5).
\end{itemize}

The following sections identify the principal remaining limitations and the
most productive paths forward for each discipline.

% ============================================================
\subsection{Neutronics}
% ============================================================

The Power Inverter strategy successfully extends the achievable critical cycle
length by inverting the axial burnup profile and directing fission power into
the under-depleted rod extremities. However, two limitations of the current
design warrant attention.

First, the fresh-fuel driver zone occupies 25 of 225 assembly positions and
accumulates weapons-grade plutonium by end of cycle. This partially
contradicts the spent-fuel-only design intent and creates a proliferation-
relevant material stream that must be managed under safeguards. A more
proliferation-resistant path forward is an optimized spent-fuel reloading
scheme in which lower-burnup spent assemblies are inserted at specific axial
or radial positions as the cycle progresses, sustaining reactivity without
introducing fresh fissile material. This is a constrained combinatorial
optimization problem amenable to genetic algorithms or adjoint-based
sensitivity methods.

Second, the end-of-cycle coefficient of variation of the axial fissile
distribution (CV\,=\,15.28\%) does not meet the $\leq$10\% uniformity
objective. Further reduction requires either enrichment gradient optimization
beyond the parameter range explored here, a modified PI-F geometry with a
more gradual enrichment transition, or the reloading strategy described above.
Meeting the CV target would also reduce the PPF, relaxing the hot-channel
thermal margin constraint.

% ============================================================
\subsection{Thermal-Hydraulics}
% ============================================================


The thermal--hydraulic results suggest that the principal path to improvement is not necessarily a reduction in average heat flux, but an increase in coolant-side heat removal capacity. The most direct lever is mass flow rate: increasing coolant flow would raise mass flux, increase Reynolds number, improve the convective heat transfer coefficient, and therefore reduce cladding temperature and increase DNBR margin. This would partly offset the thermal penalty imposed by the 6~cm pitch. A second lever is reduction of the power peaking factor through further neutronic and geometric optimization, since the hot-channel condition is clearly more limiting than the average channel.

More broadly, the thermal analysis demonstrates the principal tradeoff at the heart of the LBR concept. The large moderator pitch selected to achieve favorable breeding and operating lifetime also weakens coolant heat transfer. The spent-fuel loading strategy that improves fissile utilization also raises power peaking. These choices are not independent: the thermal design is constrained by the neutronic objectives, and the reactor cannot be judged on neutronics alone. The analytical model, once validated to the V5 CFD case, provides the practical tool needed to carry this coupled optimization forward in future iterations.

\subsection{Infrastructure and Operational Architecture}
% ============================================================

The 6\,cm lattice pitch required for adequate D$_2$O moderation results in a
core that is substantially larger than a conventional PWR of equivalent
thermal output. At 2\,GW$_\text{th}$ with approximately 19\,400 fuel rods,
the active core footprint poses challenges for pressure vessel fabrication
and reactor building layout. Adopting a pressure-tube architecture analogous
to CANDU, in which individual fuel channels are contained within their own
pressure tubes inside a low-pressure moderator tank (calandria), would
distribute the pressure boundary over many smaller components, making
fabrication more tractable and enabling individual channel access for online
or near-online refueling.

Online refueling eliminates the large initial excess reactivity inherent to
batch refueling and reduces the control burden on soluble boron or burnable
poisons. It also supports the spent-fuel reloading optimization strategy
described above. The engineering decisions that follow from this architecture
--- vessel orientation, pressure tube and calandria material selection, remote
fuel handling system design --- represent the principal scope of future
conceptual design work.

% ============================================================
\subsection{Economic Viability}
% ============================================================

At the assembly-processing level, the LBR is economically competitive with
DUPIC and substantially cheaper to fabricate, at \$501/kgHM versus
\$1,195/kgHM for DUPIC. The all-in lifecycle cost of \$1,659/kgHM compares
favorably to the \$2,353/kgHM DUPIC reference. On a marginal energy basis,
the incremental energy extracted from spent fuel costs \$110.6/MWd, less
than DUPIC (\$181.0/MWd) but not yet matching the conventional PWR reference
(\$66.3/MWd). Closing this gap to the conventional PWR would require
extracting an additional $\sim$25\,MWd/kgHM of incremental burnup --- a
target that is likely unachievable with the current design and would require
significantly higher spent fuel enrichment or an entirely different fuel
management strategy.

The economically compelling case for LBR is not direct competition with
the conventional fuel cycle, but rather the avoided cost of spent fuel
disposal. Utilities currently face a back-end cost of $\approx$\$1,195/kgHM
for direct disposal of spent PWR assemblies. By comparison, the net marginal
cost of extracting 15\,MWd/kgHM of additional energy through LBR processing
is \$464/15\,$=$\,\$31/MWd --- well below the avoided direct-disposal
benchmark. At the system level, however, these figures apply only to assembly
processing; the full cost of licensing, constructing, and operating the LBR
plant would substantially change the economic picture and is the primary area
requiring further analysis before commercial viability can be claimed.

% ============================================================
\subsection{Overall Assessment and Path Forward}
% ============================================================

The Last Breath Reactor concept is technically feasible at the analysis level
demonstrated in this report. The fundamental physics enabling the design ---
D$_2$O neutron economy and the thermal cross-section advantage of
$^{239}$Pu absorption over $^{238}$U capture --- have been rigorously
established and quantitatively verified. The Power Inverter device provides a
practical engineering solution to the axial burnup non-uniformity problem that
would otherwise cap the achievable cycle length well short of the 60\,GWd/t
target.

The most consequential unresolved constraint is the hot-channel thermal
margin: the same lattice geometry that enables plutonium burning also
degrades heat removal, and the power peaking introduced by the PI-F loading
pattern amplifies local thermal loads. Closing this gap --- through coolant
flow optimization, PPF reduction, or both --- is the single highest-priority
engineering task before the concept can advance to a detailed design phase.

The combination of reduced fabrication cost, meaningful incremental energy
extraction, and demonstrated plutonium isotopic degradation positions the LBR
as a credible alternative to both direct disposal and chemically intensive
reprocessing strategies. With continued development of the reloading
optimization, hot-channel management, and overclad qualification program, the
design provides a viable pathway toward commercial deployment.
