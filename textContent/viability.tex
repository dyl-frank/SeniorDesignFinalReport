\textbf{Neutronics}

The LBR concept has demonstrated, up to this point, the technical feasibility of sustaining criticality on spent nuclear fuel while achieving net plutonium reduction and isotopic quality degradation. The \textit{Power Inverter} strategy extends the achievable cycle length by inverting the axial burnup profile, enabling the reactor to reach an average discharge burnup of 61.6\,GWd/tHM within NRC regulatory limits. However, the Power Inverter relies on a fresh-fuel driver zone to supply the additional reactivity required at end of cycle, which introduces two concerns: it partially contradicts the design objective of operating exclusively on spent fuel, and the fresh-fuel zone accumulates weapons-grade plutonium by end of cycle, creating a proliferation-relevant material stream that must be managed.

A more proliferation-resistant and fuel-cycle-consistent path forward would be the development of an optimized spent-fuel reloading scheme. By strategically inserting lower-burnup spent fuel assemblies as the cycle progresses, it may be possible to sustain criticality beyond the current 62-GWd/t baseline without resorting to fresh fuel. Such a scheme is a constrained combinatorial optimization problem: the reloading pattern must simultaneously satisfy reactivity hold-down requirements, respect the NRC burnup ceiling on any individual assembly, maintain acceptable power peaking factors, and avoid excessive fission product buildup in the highest-burnup regions. Techniques such as in-core fuel management optimization, genetic algorithms, or adjoint-based sensitivity methods could be applied to this problem.


\textbf{Structural \& Operational}

The 6\,cm lattice pitch required for adequate D$_2$O moderation results in a core that is substantially larger than a conventional PWR of equivalent thermal output. At 2\,GW$_\text{th}$ with approximately 19\,400 fuel rods on a 6\,cm square pitch, the active core footprint is on the order of several meters in each lateral dimension, posing challenges for pressure vessel fabrication and reactor building design. To reduce the physical size of the core while maintaining neutron economy, the power density should be pushed toward the upper bound permitted by thermal-hydraulic and materials limits. A higher specific power reduces the required heavy metal inventory for a given thermal output, but accelerates fuel depletion and narrows the available operating window before the burnup ceiling is reached.

This tension between core compactness and cycle length motivates a design philosophy centered on continuous or semi-continuous on-line refueling, analogous to the approach used in CANDU reactors. On-line refueling eliminates the need to carry large initial excess reactivity, allowing the core to operate closer to criticality throughout the cycle and reducing the control burden on soluble boron or burnable poisons. Key engineering decisions that follow from this include the choice of vessel orientation (vertical versus horizontal), the use of pressure tubes rather than a single pressure vessel to enable individual channel access, and the development of a remote fuel handling system compatible with the D$_2$O coolant and elevated radiation environment. Each of these choices carries significant implications for primary system layout, maintenance dose, and capital cost, and represents a major area of future design work.
